## 1 · Catch the drift before the meeting, not at it · 0:45

**TFGPT lets final-year IE students catch drift in their TFG before the deliverable meeting, not at it.**

The students we heard from find out at the meeting that their work no longer fits the guidelines or their topic. By then it's done, and they redo it.

TFGPT knows their programme's guidelines and flags what doesn't fit. It never grades and never drafts.

How proven is it? 6 of 10 students did the check in our chat instead of their own AI. On one real proposal, our stand-in caught everything the advisor flagged. Whether IE will back it is untested.

---

## 2 · The only real check is the meeting · 1:30

**Today the only real check is a supervisor with about ten TFGs and a few minutes for each.**

Here's what students told us they do. They draft with their own AI, judge it themselves, rewrite it so it doesn't read as AI, and hope it holds. Some find out at the meeting and redo the work since the last one. In the Computer Science programme we heard about, deliverables are about two months apart. Other programmes have different dates, so the gap varies.

The advisor side may explain why. One advisor has about ten TFGs and a few minutes per student, and some of his students told us they waited almost two weeks for a first reply. A second advisor, with two TFGs, reportedly has long conversations. So the load may matter, but we haven't tested that.

It's a small base: one graduate talking about his classmates, a few final-year students, and two advisors. Our personas are built from it, and they're hypotheses, not evidence.

---

## 3 · One risk, one cheap test · 1:15

**Our riskiest bet: students will do the pre-meeting check in TFGPT instead of their own AI.**

If that's false, nothing else matters. So we tested exactly that, before building anything.

A Wizard of Oz: a simple chat loaded with the student's topic and the IE guidelines, and a teammate answering live behind it. Ten final-year students, four degrees, 20 minutes each, last week of September. Three tasks: does my topic fit, what are the format rules, and when are the dates.

Before running it, we wrote down the bar: at least 6 of 10 do all three tasks in our chat without opening their own AI. If met, we continue. If missed, we reframe TFGPT as a complement to their AI.

Our landing page is a signal, not a test: 19 people signed up. 2 said they'd use it instead of their own AI, 11 alongside it, and 6 probably not. Most see it next to their own AI, which is how we position it. We set no bar for it, and it went to the same class group as our test students. 

---

## 4 · What happened and what it changed · 1:30

**6 of 10, exactly on our bar, so we continue. The other four taught us more.**

Exactly on the bar is medium-strength evidence: one 20-minute session with ten classmates. Two of the six also had thin sessions: one hadn't chosen a topic yet, and one had already finished the proposal.

Of the other four:
- Two used their own AI alongside our chat. One worked out answers in ChatGPT; the other generated topic ideas in Claude and brought them to us to check.
- One did every task in Claude and only pasted the answers back.
- One skipped a task.

Separately, 4 of 10 double-checked what mattered with Blackboard, a friend, Google or their supervisor. And one student spent about 8 minutes on whether her topic was too mathematical. That's the drift question itself.

What the test changed: TFGPT is used instead of their AI for the check, and alongside it for everything else. And because students double-checked with official sources, every flag cites the guideline section. No grades came from our desk research.

## 5 · Our real rival is the AI they already use · 1:15

**Our real rival isn't a thesis tool. It's the ChatGPT or Claude students already use.**

That includes the AI IE itself hands out: IE AI Engine and ChatGPT Edu. There are direct competitors too: Grammarly's AI grader, Thesify, and Mattie, a pilot at RSM in Rotterdam. Then there's the manual route (Blackboard, friends, emailing the supervisor), or doing nothing and accepting the rework. None we found checks work against the student's own university guidelines and keeps their context.

The size: about 270 IE students a year, out of 1,351 graduates. The 60% step is our own test result, from one session of ten, so it's the least certain input. That's why the range runs from 65 to 650.

So the prize is a pilot at IE, about 270 students a year. We haven't priced it, because willingness to pay is untested.

## 6 · Own the pre-meeting check · 1:15

**We don't try to be a better AI. We own one job: the check before the meeting.**

The supervisor knows the guidelines but is slow. Their own AI is fast but doesn't know their programme. No alternative scores above 3 out of 5 on keeping your context or checking scope before the meeting, and none above 1 on starting the check by itself. Those scores are our judgement, not measurements.

Four moves:
- **Eliminate** grades and verdicts: we flag, the supervisor decides.
- **Reduce** writing to fixes for flagged passages: students keep writing with their own AI.
- **Raise** programme knowledge and kept context: no uploading, no re-briefing, one place for the whole TFG.
- **Create** a check that starts itself before each deliverable.

Create is our least-evidenced move. It came from a persona hypothesis, so it's not in the first slice.

## 7 · Our spike: two routes, two real advisors · 1:30

**On one real proposal, our stand-in flagged all 9 points the advisor had raised.**

**Team member 1:** I checked my proposal with a TFGPT stand-in: a Claude project with every programme document, and instructions to flag what might be wrong and never write a sentence. My advisor made 4 corrections, all minor: punctuation, one word, and one passage that was too technical.

**Team member 2:** I used a plain Claude chat with no instructions and only the documents I could find. My advisor made 9 corrections, mostly major: the methodology and the research question. Then I ran the same proposal through team member 1's stand-in. It flagged all 9, and my revised version came back with small fixes only.

**Team member 1:** The limits. Two different people, proposals and advisors, so 4 against 9 isn't a fair race. And we matched the flags after we'd seen the advisor's list, not blind. It shows the mechanism can work, not that students want it.

## 8 · Continue: more students, then advisors · 1:30

**We continue, but we build a narrow slice, not the product.**

The first slice: one programme's guidelines, the check with cited flags, and kept context.

Two next tests, each with a bar we set first:
1. The same comparison with more students who are willing to try it, by 23 October. If the stand-in misses more than 40% of what their advisors flag, we stop and narrow the check to what it flags reliably.
2. The advisor side, which we haven't tested at all: two or three advisors see a mock-up. If none would use it, we drop it.

**Would anyone invest? Yes: IE, as a pilot.** Students and advisors told us about the pain, 6 of 10 did the check in our chat, and on one real proposal the stand-in caught all 9 advisor points. That yes holds only if IE commits to keeping the guidelines current, and nobody at IE has been asked yet. That's the part of our yes we haven't earned. Thank you.

## Questions we expect (one breath each)

- **6 of 10 is exactly your bar. Luck?** Could be. One student behaving differently would have missed it. We set the bar before the test. Repeating it in real use, over a whole deliverable, is still open.
- **Why not just paste the guidelines into ChatGPT?** That's the biggest open question. On slide 7, a plain chat missed what the advisor flagged and our stand-in caught it. But that chat had only part of the documents and no instructions. If a student who pastes everything gets the same flags for the same effort, we stop or pivot.
- **Did it write your proposal?** Not in the stand-in route: it was built never to write sentences, and all that text is ours. Both runs count as AI use, so both belong in our TFG AI declarations.
- **How many wrong or extra flags?** We only checked that it caught the 9. We didn't count extra or wrong flags this time; the next test does.
- **Who pays?** We mean IE, as the sponsor of a pilot. It isn't priced and nobody at IE has been asked. Students paying looks weak, because free tools exist.
- **IE already gives everyone ChatGPT. Why fund a second tool?** Only if TFGPT gives the same flags for less effort. If ChatGPT matches us, IE has no reason to, and we stop or pivot.
- **What counts as IE committing?** Three things: a named owner who keeps the guidelines current, access to them, and the tool offered to final-year students.
- **Isn't this an AI grader? What about the EU AI Act?** No grades or verdicts, only flags with the guideline section. Our legal position is unknown and needs advice.
- **Are your personas real?** They're built from our evidence with AI and not validated with real users, so they never count as evidence.
- **What could make you narrow, pivot or stop?** These are options, not decisions yet:
  - Misses more than 40% of what advisors flag: we narrow the check.
  - Students won't switch in real use: we reframe it as a complement.
  - ChatGPT gives the same flags for the same effort: we stop or pivot.
  - IE won't commit: we narrow to one programme or advisor who keeps the guidelines current, or we drop the IE-backed version.
- **My programme meets monthly. Two months?** That's the Computer Science programme we heard about. The aim is to check before each programme's own dates, but that self-starting part isn't built or tested yet.
