# Job to Be Done

**Status:** Working hypothesis requiring real-user validation

**Sources:** interview-evidence.md (01: an IE Computer Science graduate; 02: informal reports from other students)

## Job to Be Done statement

When final-year IE students work on their TFG through two-month gaps between deliverable meetings without knowing whether they're on track, finding out at a meeting that the last two months of work were faulty prompts them to seek a way to go into each meeting already knowing their work meets what's required, so they can move on to the next phase

## Job to Be Done components

| Lens | Element | Working definition | Basis or status |
|---|---|---|---|
| How the job begins | Circumstance | Working on the TFG through about two months between deliverables, without knowing whether the work is on track | Supported: reported by an IE Computer Science graduate (his own and his classmates' experience) |
| How the job begins | Trigger | Finding out at the deliverable meeting that the last two months of work were faulty, and having to redo it using the next deliverable's time | Supported, second-hand: his classmates' experience, as he reported it |
| Progress | Desired progress | Go into each deliverable meeting already knowing the work meets what's required, and move on to the next phase instead of redoing the last one | Supported: his own words |
| Progress | Solution hired | Their own AI drafts the thesis. They judge for themselves whether it's right, rewrite it so it isn't flagged as AI, then hope it holds at the meeting. Contrast case: the graduate also had close supervisor guidance and was less affected | Supported, second-hand. "Almost everyone" is an impression, not a count |
| Forces compelling change | Push | Two months of work thrown away, the next deliverable's time spent redoing it, extra thesis hours on top of other subjects, missed deadlines | Supported: reported, partly second-hand |
| Forces compelling change | Pull | Knowing before the meeting whether the work matches what's expected, instead of eyeballing it and hoping | Team interpretation |
| Forces opposing change | Habit | They stay with the AI they already use for almost everything (paid plans for most Computer Science students, free or cheapest versions for most others) because it's the best they know | Supported: reported by students. The payment split by degree isn't checked |
| Forces opposing change | Anxiety | Worry that a new option isn't good enough, because tools the university offers tend to run old, outdated models | Supported: reported directly by students |

**Relevant progress dimensions:** Functional (move on to the next phase instead of redoing work; the thesis doesn't take time from other subjects), Emotional (peace of mind, not being caught out at the meeting)

## Validation gaps

- All of the evidence comes through one IE Computer Science graduate and what the student has heard. It needs checking with more final-year students across different degrees
- It's unclear whether students feel anything is wrong during the two months, or only after a bad meeting. The job may only start once someone has been caught out
- The scale claims ("almost no one finished on time", "almost everyone had AI write it") haven't been checked
- Pull is the student's interpretation, not something students said
- "It would be a no-brainer if it compared fairly to their own AI" is a stated intention about switching, not behaviour
- It's open whether "relying more on the tool they use" means their current AI or a new tool
